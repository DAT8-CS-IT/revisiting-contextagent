## Info
- **Title:** When AI-Based Agents Are Proactive: Implications for Competence and System Satisfaction in Human–AI Collaboration  
- **Authors & Year:** Christopher Diebel, Marc Goutier, Martin Adam, Alexander Benlian (2025)  
- **Venue:** *Business & Information Systems Engineering*  

# 2. Problem Addressed
The paper examines how proactive (vs. reactive) AI help affects users’ feelings of competence and satisfaction when working with AI systems. This matters because proactive AI is becoming more common, yet its psychological effects on users are poorly understood. Prior work largely focused on performance benefits while ignoring how different help‑initiation styles shape user experience and willingness to collaborate with AI.

# 3. Key Contributions
- Shows that proactive AI help lowers system satisfaction compared to reactive help.  
- Identifies loss of competence-based self-esteem as the key mediator.  
- Demonstrates that AI knowledge amplifies this negative effect.  
- Introduces help-initiation style as an important design principle for AI systems.  
- Provides practical guidance on when to use proactive vs. reactive AI.

# 4. Method / Approach
The authors use self-determination theory to explain why proactive help may threaten users’ sense of competence. They run a vignette-based online experiment with 92 programmers, randomly assigning them to proactive or reactive AI-help scenarios. Participants watched video mockups of an AI coding assistant either offering help spontaneously or only after being asked. They then reported their sense of competence, satisfaction, and other measures. The authors used OLS regressions and PROCESS mediation/moderation analyses to test their hypotheses. The model assessed direct, indirect, and moderated effects to uncover how and why help-initiation changes user experience.

# 5. Results & Evidence
Across conditions, proactive help significantly reduced system satisfaction (about 1.08 points on a 7‑point scale). It also caused a measurable decline in competence-based self-esteem, which in turn reduced satisfaction. Users with higher AI knowledge reacted more negatively—showing stronger competence loss and lower satisfaction—while low‑knowledge users showed no significant impact. The evidence supports a moderated‑mediation model: proactive help → competence loss → lower satisfaction, amplified by AI knowledge.

# 6. Strengths & Limitations
**Strengths:**  
- Clear causal identification through controlled experimental design.  
- Strong theoretical grounding in self-determination theory.  
- Practical relevance for AI tool designers and organizations.

**Limitations:**  
- Single context (programming) limits generalizability.  
- Vignette design lacks the richness of real-world interaction.  
- Cultural and domain diversity not explored.  
- Only competence (not autonomy or relatedness) is examined.

# 7. Essential Takeaways
- Proactivity in AI isn’t always appreciated—even helpful behavior can feel competence-threatening.  
- Users care as much about *how* AI helps as *what* it delivers.  
- AI knowledge can backfire, making proactive AI feel more threatening.  
- Designing AI requires balancing productivity gains with psychological comfort.  
- Allowing users to control or opt into proactivity can improve acceptance.  
- Competence-supportive design (e.g., positive framing, optional suggestions) matters.  
- Help-initiation style should be treated as a core HCI/Human–AI collaboration design variable.

# 8. One-Sentence Essence
Proactive AI help may boost efficiency, but it risks undermining users’ sense of competence—and thus their satisfaction—especially among those who understand AI best.
